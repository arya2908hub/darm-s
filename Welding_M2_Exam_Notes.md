## 2) Manufacturing Basics and Classification (Slides 5-10)
### 2.1 What is Manufacturing?
- Manufacturing (from Latin roots) is conversion of **raw materials** into **useful products**.

### 2.2 Classification by Mass Change
- **Mass reducing** \((dM<0)\): material removed.
- **Mass adding** \((dM>0)\): material added.
- **Mass conserving** \((dM\approx 0)\): shape changed mainly without significant mass change.

### 2.3 Typical Process Families Mentioned
- Mass reducing:
  - Conventional machining: turning, milling, drilling, grinding
  - Non-conventional machining
- Mass adding:
  - Additive manufacturing
  - Powder metallurgy
- Mass conserving:
  - Casting, metal forming, joining processes

### 2.4 Why Non-conventional Machining?
- Machining **hard/ceramic materials**
- Producing **complex geometries** and **micro-features**

### 4.1 Why special joining for metals?
- Adhesives and common fasteners may not be adequate for many structural metal-joint demands.

## 5) Welding Fundamentals (Slides 24-26)
### 5.1 Definition and Principle
- Welding is a **permanent joining process** where metals are joined by heating (and sometimes pressure) to create coalescence.
- Filler may be:
  - supplied by electrode itself, or
  - supplied separately.
- Similar and dissimilar metals may be joined.
- Typical stated high-temperature range in arc context: about **6000 to 7000 degC**.

### 5.2 Heat Sources for Welding
1. Chemical reaction
2. Electric arc
3. Electrical resistance
4. Frictional heat

### 5.3 Broad Welding Classification
- **Plastic/pressure welding**:
  - Metals heated to plastic state and forced together by pressure
  - generally without filler
  - examples: forge welding, resistance welding
- **Fusion welding**:
  - no pressure required
  - joint region melted and resolidified
  - filler may or may not be added
  - examples: oxy-acetylene, carbon arc welding

### 7.1 Welded Joints

### 7.2 Arc Welding Principle
- Fusion welding method where arc heat melts the joint region.
- Arc exists between electrode (anode/cathode role shown) and base metal.
- Electrical energy -> thermal energy.
- Arc temperature cited around **6000 to 7000 degC**.

### 7.3 Electrodes
- **Non-consumable**: carbon/graphite/tungsten.
- **Consumable**: typically steel wire/rod; may be bare or flux-coated.
- Flux-coated electrode protection mechanisms (SMAW context):
  - slag cover over weld pool
  - shielding gases generated by flux decomposition

## 8) Arc Welding Processes (Slides 41-53)
## 8.1 Manual Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) (slide 42 + figure slides)
- Consumable flux-coated electrode provides filler.
- High localized heat input; narrow HAZ relative to heat concentration statement.
- Widely used due to versatility and low setup cost.
- Manual process -> quality strongly depends on welder skill.
- Electrode dimensions mentioned: approx. diameter 1.6 to 6.3 mm; length 250 to 450 mm.
- Power sources:
  - AC transformer
  - DC generator/rectifier
- Suitable for wide plate-thickness range and many commercial metals/alloys.
- Usable in all welding positions.

### 8.2 Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW/MIG) (slides 45-46)
- Continuous consumable wire acts as current carrier + filler.
- Arc between wire and workpiece.
- External shielding gas: Ar, CO2, or mixtures.
- Common applications: automobile, sheet-metal fabrication, pressure vessels.
- Suitable thickness: roughly **1 mm to 25 mm**.
- Arc temperature: roughly **6500 to 7500 degC**.
- Typical source: **CV DC**, usually **DCEP**.

### 8.3 Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW/TIG) (slides 47-48)
- Arc between non-consumable tungsten electrode and workpiece.
- Filler added separately when needed.
- Shielding by inert gas (argon/helium).
- Precision applications: aerospace, nuclear, chemical sectors.
- Suitable especially for thin sections: approx. **0.5 to 6 mm** (up to ~10 mm by multipass).
- Arc temperature can reach ~**11,000 degC**.
- Typical source: **CC**;
  - AC for Al/Mg
  - DC for steels/other metals.

### 8.4 Submerged Arc Welding (SAW) (slides 49-50)
- Arc and weld pool submerged under granular flux blanket.
- Benefits: reduced spatter, reduced arc radiation, good pool protection.
- Used for thick plates: approx. **10 mm to 100 mm**.
- Arc temperature: ~**7000 to 8000 degC**.
- Source: **CV**, AC or DC depending on use.
- Typical applications: pressure vessels, large pipes, tanks, ship hulls.

### 8.5 Plasma Arc Welding (PAW) (slides 51-52)
- Constricted plasma arc through narrow nozzle -> high energy density.
- High precision and repeatability.
- Suitable thickness: roughly **0.1 mm to 8 mm**.
- Plasma arc temperature: about **16,000 to 20,000 degC**.
- Power source: **CC DC**.
- Applications: aerospace, medical devices, precision industries.

### 8.6 Arc Welding: Advantages, Disadvantages, Applications (slide 53)
**Advantages**
- Simple setup, portable equipment
- Can weld most metals/alloys
- Good weld quality achievable

**Disadvantages**
- Slower process (in context of production alternatives)
- Post-cleaning needed (slag removal)

**Applications**
- Tanks, boilers, pressure vessels, shipbuilding, pipe joining

## 9) Metal Transfer Modes and Power Source Logic (Slides 54-58)
### 9.1 Metal Transfer Modes
- Short circuit
- Globular
- Spray
- Pulse spray
- Rotating spray

### 9.2 Governing Forces Mentioned
- Electromagnetic pinch force
- Surface tension force
- Drag force
- Gravitational force

### 9.3 Deposition-Rate Table (as listed)
- Short circuit: **0.9 to 2.7 kg/hr**
- Globular: **1.8 to 3.2 kg/hr**
- Spray: **2.7 to 5.4 kg/hr**
- Pulse spray: **0.9 to 2.7 kg/hr**
- Rotating spray: **6.9 to 13.5 kg/hr**

### 9.4 Power Source Types
- **Constant Current (CC):** current stable, voltage varies with arc length
  - suited to manual arc-length processes (SMAW, GTAW)
- **Constant Voltage (CV):** voltage stable, current varies with wire-feed behavior
  - suited to wire-feed processes (MIG/MAG, SAW)

## 10) Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM) (Slides 59-81)

- WAAM variants by arc process:

### 10.5 GMAW-Based WAAM Details (slides 67-76)
#### (a) Polarity/heat distribution concept
- Slides discuss DCEN, DCEP, and AC with different heat-distribution statements between electrode and substrate.
- Includes penetration/deposited bead visuals.

> Ambiguity note: polarity labels and heat-split statements appear partly inconsistent on one slide; preserve with caution.

#### (b) Metal transfer modes reused in WAAM context
- Same mode names/rates as earlier arc-welding section.

#### (c) Cold Metal Transfer (CMT)
- Popular WAAM approach due to controlled heat input and reduced spatter.
- Attributed to Fronius (2003).
- Process cycle:
  1. Arc starts with main current and wire feeding.
  2. Droplet forms and wire dips into melt pool.
  3. Current is reduced for controlled transfer.
  4. Wire retracts after transfer.
  5. Main current reapplied; cycle repeats.

#### (d) Process parameters and experiment hierarchy (slides 71, 73)
- Parameter groups include:
  - Power/current/voltage (starting, main, end current)
  - Filler wire type/material/feed rate
  - Gas composition, flow rate, pressure, pre/post flow
  - Torch speed, stick-out, standoff
  - Toolpath strategy and step-over
- Experimental levels:
  - **Single bead:** penetration, height, contact angle, profile
  - **Multi-bead:** step-over, flatness, layer-height control
  - **Multi-layer:** tensile, wear, hardness variation, fatigue/impact

#### (e) Bead-profile modeling (slide 72)
- Bead profile above substrate assumed symmetric parabola:
  \[
  y = a + c x^2
  \]
  with
  \[
  a = h, \quad c = -\frac{4h}{w^2}
  \]
- Here, \(h\) = bead height, \(w\) = bead width.

#### (f) Low-carbon steel multilayer behavior
- Top layers may show higher hardness.
- Repeated thermal cycling can lower core hardness.
- Toolpath significantly affects hardness distribution.
- Heat treatment suggested for hardness uniformity.

#### (g) Tensile testing and trends
- ASTM E8 sub-size specimen reference.
- Samples should include:
  - deposition direction
  - build direction
  - transverse direction
- At least three samples suggested.
- Higher welding current -> higher tensile strength (via penetration), but trade-off with layer height/surface accuracy.

### 10.6 GTAW- and PAW-based WAAM (slides 77-79)
- GTAW WAAM: stainless steel/titanium deposition examples; DCEN/AC noted.
- PAW WAAM: equipment modules shown (chiller, wire feeders, plasma/welding unit, robotic setup).
- GTAW parameter matrix shown similar to GMAW parameter framework.

### 10.7 Gas effects and WAAM challenges (slides 80-81)
#### Gas effects (as listed)
- **Argon:** stable arc, smooth bead, oxidation control for non-ferrous materials.
- **CO2:** low cost, better penetration, but more spatter.
- **Ar-CO2 mix:** balanced stability/penetration with less spatter than pure CO2.
- **Helium (often with Ar):** higher thermal conductivity -> higher heat input, penetration, and welding speed.

#### WAAM challenges
1. Surface-quality difficulty (heat distribution, arc instability, spatter buildup)
2. Residual stress/distortion due to thermal cycling
3. Material/feedstock limitations and weldability constraints
4. Need to minimize porosity, lack of fusion, and bead-shape irregularity
5. Support-structure design challenges for complex overhangs
6. Limited standardization/certification (emerging technology)
